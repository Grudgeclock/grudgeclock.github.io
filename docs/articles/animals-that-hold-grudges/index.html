Animals that hold grudges, and how long they keep them

By Built by ArgoPublished 29 October 20267 min read

Short answer

Yes, some animals hold something very close to a grudge. Crows hold the record: a group of Seattle crows scolded one researcher's mask for about 17 years. Ravens avoid people who cheated them, elephants react to the voices of people who hunt them, and chimpanzees retaliate against thieves. Dogs and cats remember bad experiences, but there is little evidence of a grudge in the human sense.

Whether an animal can "hold a grudge" depends on what you count. Scientists are careful here: a grudge in the human sense involves resentment and a sense of being wronged, which is hard to measure in an animal. What can be measured is behaviour. Does the animal remember a specific individual who harmed it? Does it act against them later? By that standard, a handful of species qualify.

Crows: the 17-year grudge

Trapped seven crows while wearing a fearsome mask

≈17 years

University of Washington, Seattle. Scolding peaked around 2013 and stopped by September 2023.

In 2006, University of Washington wildlife scientist John Marzluff and his team wore a fearsome mask while they trapped seven crows, fitted them with leg rings and let them go. Afterwards, people walked the University of Washington campus wearing the same mask. Crows scolded them: loud, harsh calls, sometimes mobbing the walker.

As a control, other walkers wore a neutral mask with the face of Dick Cheney, then the US vice president. They fed the crows and were left alone.

The striking part is what happened next. More crows joined in over the years, including birds that had never been trapped. A 2012 study by the same group showed why: crows learn who is dangerous from each other, and young crows pick up the scolding from their parents. Scolding peaked around 2013, seven years in. Then it slowly faded. On a masked walk in September 2023, about 17 years after the trapping, not one crow scolded.

The later data hadn't been published in a journal when it was reported, so treat the exact 17-year figure as the researchers' own observation. Even so, it is the longest documented grudge we know of in any animal.

Ravens: they remember who cheated them

Ravens are crows' larger cousins, and in 2017 researchers from the University of Vienna and Lund University tested whether they remember unfairness. They trained ravens to trade a piece of bread for a piece of cheese with human partners. One partner traded fairly. Another took the bread and ate the cheese themselves.

A month later, the ravens could choose between the two. They avoided the cheat. Ravens that had only watched the unfair trade, without being cheated themselves, didn't show the same preference. The researchers think ravens could remember such people for much longer, possibly years, based on how long they remember other ravens, but that wasn't tested.

Elephants: they know who hunts them

"An elephant never forgets" is a cliché, but there is evidence behind a version of it. In Amboseli National Park in Kenya, researchers played recordings of human voices to wild elephant families. Some voices belonged to Maasai men, who sometimes spear elephants. Others belonged to Kamba men, who pose much less of a threat.

The elephants reacted more defensively to the Maasai men's voices, bunching together and sniffing the air, than to the Kamba voices or to Maasai women and boys. They could tell ethnicity, sex and age from a voice alone. This isn't a grudge against one person, but it is a lasting memory of who means danger.

Chimpanzees: vengeful, but not spiteful

In a 2007 study published in PNAS, chimpanzees could collapse a table to send food out of another chimpanzee's reach. They mostly didn't bother when the other chimp had got the food by chance. They did it when the other chimp had stolen the food from them.

The researchers' title sums it up: chimpanzees are vengeful but not spiteful. They punish what is done to them, but don't punish others just for having more.

Do dogs hold grudges?

Not in the way people do, as far as anyone can tell. But they do notice how people treat their owners. In a 2015 study at Kyoto University, dogs watched their owner ask a stranger for help opening a container. In one version, the stranger turned away. Afterwards, the stranger and a neutral person both offered the dog a treat.

Dogs that had seen their owner rebuffed were more likely to take the treat from the neutral person, avoiding the unhelpful one. When the stranger had helped, or the situation was neutral, the dogs chose at random. The study tested this right after the interaction, so it says little about how long a dog remembers.

Do cats hold grudges?

The evidence says no, at least not as people do. Cats form strong associations: a cat that had a bad experience with a carrier, a person or a room may avoid it for a long time. That avoidance can look like a grudge. Animal welfare charity Cats Protection's view is that cats don't hold grudges in the same way humans do, and that grudge-like behaviour is usually fear or stress tied to a memory. There is simply very little research on cat grudges specifically.

Which animal holds the longest grudge?

On the evidence available, crows. The Seattle crows' roughly 17-year campaign against a single mask is the longest documented case of an animal acting against a specific individual it learned to associate with harm. Ravens may rival them, but the long-term version hasn't been tested yet.

Humans, for the record, have kept grudges for centuries. See the longest grudges in history.
