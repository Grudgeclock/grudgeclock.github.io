How long can you hold a grudge?

By Built by ArgoPublished 29 October 20266 min read

Short answer

As long as you like. There is no natural limit: people have kept grudges for decades and families have kept them for generations. On average, though, the urge to get even fades as time passes. What keeps a grudge going is mostly rumination, replaying what happened, and what shortens it is often an apology.

We make an app that times grudges to the second, so we think about this question more than is healthy. Here is what the research says, and what the record books say.

Do grudges fade on their own?

Usually, yes. The best-known work on this comes from psychologist Michael McCullough and colleagues, who followed people over time after someone had hurt them and measured two things: the urge to avoid that person and the urge to get back at them. In their 2003 study, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, those urges typically declined after the offence. Later researchers describe the pattern as forgiveness rising steadily over time, on average.

"On average" is doing a lot of work. The same research shows large differences between people and between offences. Some people let go quickly. Some don't let go at all. Which brings us to the interesting part.

What keeps a grudge alive

Rumination

In 2007, McCullough, Bono and Root ran three longitudinal studies on rumination: going over an offence again and again in your head. On days when people ruminated more than usual, they were less forgiving than usual. The link ran through anger, not fear, and the timing suggested that rumination came first and the drop in forgiveness followed.

In plain terms: a grudge you keep thinking about stays fresh. A grudge you stop thinking about tends to cool.

No apology, no amends

A 2014 study in PNAS followed 337 people who had recently been hurt by someone close to them. Apologies and offers of compensation went together with faster forgiveness and less anger. The authors think these gestures work by changing how the victim sees the offender: as more valuable to keep around, and less likely to do it again. The study was correlational, so it shows a link rather than proof of cause.

Keeping contact with the source

Common sense, and the research above, point the same way: a grudge against someone you see every week gets topped up. "Said my haircut was brave" lasts longer when the person who said it sits across from you at lunch.

Can you hold a grudge forever?

Long enough that it looks like it. A few documented cases, timed the way Grudge Clock would time them:

The Dassler brothers (Adidas vs. Puma)

61 years

From the 1948 split to a joint employee football match in 2009. Rudolf and Adi Dassler died in 1974 and 1978 without making up, and are buried at opposite ends of the same cemetery.

The Hatfields and the McCoys

125 years

From the 1878 dispute over a hog to a formal truce signed by descendants in 2003.

Seattle's crows vs. one researcher's mask

≈17 years

Crows scolded the mask from 2006 until 2023. Not a human grudge, but it shows memory isn't the limit.

How long should you hold a grudge?

We are not going to answer that. Grudge Clock deliberately doesn't tell anyone to forgive, move on or breathe deeply. Some grudges are about things that were genuinely wrong. Some are about leftovers.

What the research does suggest is that the length of a grudge is not fixed by the offence. It depends a lot on what you do with it: how often you go back over it, and whether anything changes on the other side. If you want to know where you stand, the honest first step is to measure it.

How to measure yours

  1. Write down what happened, in a few words.
  2. Work out when it started. Be honest. Backdating is allowed; most long grudges are.
  3. Count from that moment. In years, months and days, not a rough guess. "About a year" is often closer to two.

You can do this on paper. Grudge Clock does it with a live clock on each grudge, milestones at one week, one month and one year, and stats that add up the total. If you resolve one, its final time is frozen in an archive, which is useful the next time someone says you never let anything go.
