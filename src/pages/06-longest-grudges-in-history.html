The longest grudges in history, timed

By Built by ArgoPublished 29 October 20268 min read

Short answer

The longest grudge on record is between Rome and Carthage: the Third Punic War ended in 146 BC without a peace treaty, and the two cities' mayors only signed one in 1985, roughly 2,130 years later. Next come the Netherlands and the Isles of Scilly, whose so-called war ran from 1651 to 1986: 335 years.

We make an app that times grudges to the second, so we timed history's. Some of these are wars that nobody remembered to end; some are family feuds that outlived everyone who started them. One is arguably still running. We've noted where the sources disagree, which is more often than the trivia sites admit.

1. Rome vs. Carthage: about 2,130 years

Rome vs. Carthage

≈2,130 years

From the end of the Third Punic War in 146 BC to a symbolic treaty on 5 February 1985.

Rome and Carthage fought three wars between 264 and 146 BC. The third ended with Rome destroying Carthage. There was no peace treaty, because there was nobody left to sign one.

In 1985, the mayor of Rome, Ugo Vetere, and the mayor of Carthage, by then a suburb of Tunis, Chedli Klibi, signed a symbolic treaty of friendship. It is usually described as ending the war 2,131 years late. By a strict count it is 2,130, since there is no year zero between 1 BC and AD 1. The treaty was symbolic, of course: the fighting had stopped about 2,130 years earlier.

2. The Netherlands vs. the Isles of Scilly: 335 years

Netherlands vs. Isles of Scilly

335 years

From 30 March 1651 to 17 April 1986. No shots fired, no casualties.

During the English Civil War, the Isles of Scilly, off the tip of Cornwall, were held by Royalists whose ships raided Dutch shipping. In 1651 the Dutch admiral Maarten Tromp sailed there to demand compensation. When none came, he is said to have declared war on the islands. The Royalists surrendered to Parliament soon after, the Dutch fleet left, and nobody formally ended anything.

In 1986, Roy Duncan, chairman of the Isles of Scilly Council, wrote to the Dutch embassy in London to ask whether the islands were still at war. Embassy staff found that no peace treaty had ever been signed, and Duncan invited the Dutch ambassador, Rein Huydecoper, to the islands to end it officially. Peace was declared on 17 April 1986. The ambassador joked that it must have been horrifying for the islanders to know the Dutch could have attacked at any moment.

Historians doubt the war was ever legal: Tromp was an admiral, not a government, and Scilly was part of England. But nobody had made peace either.

3. The Campbells vs. the MacDonalds: still going

No hawkers or Campbells

334 years and counting

Since the Massacre of Glencoe, 13 February 1692 (23 February in today's calendar). The clock above is live.

Early on 13 February 1692, government soldiers who had been quartered with the MacDonalds of Glencoe, in the Scottish Highlands, turned on their hosts and killed about 30 of them. The soldiers belonged to the Earl of Argyll's regiment and were commanded by Captain Robert Campbell of Glenlyon. The betrayal of hospitality made the massacre notorious, and the Campbell name has carried the blame ever since.

The Clachaig Inn in Glencoe still has a sign that reads "No hawkers or Campbells". It's a joke, by now. But it's a joke that has been running for over three centuries, which in our book makes it the longest grudge still on public display.

Scotland used the Julian calendar in 1692, so 13 February then was 23 February in the calendar we use now. The live clock counts from the latter.

4. The Shim and Yoon clans of Korea: roughly 250 to 400 years

This one is about a hill with two graves on it. Yoon Gwan, a Goryeo general who died in 1111, was buried there; his grave was later lost. In 1662, Shim Ji-won, a prime minister of the Joseon dynasty, was buried on the same hill. When the Yoon clan rediscovered their ancestor's tomb a few metres downhill in the 1700s, they demanded the Shim grave be moved.

In 1764, King Yeongjo held a hearing and ordered both graves to be left where they were. The families kept fighting anyway, and the elders involved were punished. The dispute ended with an agreement to move the Shim family's graves to new land, but accounts give different years for it, from 2005 to 2008. Sources also disagree on the start: some count from 1614, others from the 1700s rediscovery. Either way, it ran for centuries.

5. The Hatfields vs. the McCoys: 125 years

The Hatfields vs. the McCoys

125 years

From the 1878 dispute over a hog to a truce signed on 14 June 2003.

America's best-known family feud ran along the border between West Virginia and Kentucky. Tensions between the two families date back to the Civil War, but the feud proper is usually counted from 1878, when they went to court over who owned a hog. It escalated into killings that left at least a dozen people dead.

On 14 June 2003, descendants of both families met in Pikeville, Kentucky, and signed a formal truce. The governors of Kentucky and West Virginia declared the day Hatfield and McCoy Reconciliation Day.

6. Adidas vs. Puma, the Dassler brothers: 61 years

Adolf and Rudolf Dassler

61 years

From the 1948 split to a joint football match on 21 September 2009.

Brothers Adolf (Adi) and Rudolf Dassler made shoes together in Herzogenaurach, in Bavaria, for more than 30 years, until they fell out around the Second World War. Why exactly is still debated. In 1948 they split the company: Rudolf founded what became Puma, Adi founded Adidas, with their factories on opposite banks of the river Aurach.

The town divided with them, and became known as "the town of bent necks", because people looked at each other's shoes before deciding whom they were talking to. Rudolf died in 1974 and Adi in 1978, never reconciled, and they are buried at opposite ends of the same cemetery. In 2009, staff of both companies played a football match together, the first joint event since the split, which is generally treated as the official end.

7. Seattle's crows vs. one mask: about 17 years

Not every long grudge is human. In 2006, researchers at the University of Washington trapped seven crows while wearing a fearsome mask. Crows scolded anyone wearing that mask for years, other crows learned to join in, and the scolding only died out in 2023. It's the longest documented animal grudge we know of. Not on the list: Berwick-upon-Tweed vs. Russia

You may have read that the English town of Berwick-upon-Tweed is still at war with Russia, because it was named in the declaration of the Crimean War but left out of the peace treaty. It isn't. The story goes back to around 1914, and the town's own historians call it a romantic fiction: Berwick wasn't named separately in the declaration of war either. A grudge needs a start date.

What the long ones have in common

Most of these lasted not because anyone stayed furious for centuries, but because nobody did the paperwork. Wars ended in practice and continued on paper. Families kept a dispute going long after the people who started it had died. The grudge became part of who they were. Then, eventually, someone signed something, played a football match, or put up a sign as a joke.

Which is a reasonable argument for writing down when yours started.
