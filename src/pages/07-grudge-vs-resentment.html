Grudge vs. resentment: what's the difference?

By Built by ArgoPublished 29 October 20265 min read

Short answer

Resentment is the feeling: anger or bitterness at having been wronged. A grudge is what you get when that feeling settles on someone in particular and stays. You can feel resentment about a situation for an afternoon; you hold a grudge against a person, for something specific, for a long time.

The two words are close enough that dictionaries use each to define the other. But they aren't interchangeable, and the differences are useful once you see them. Put briefly: resentment is the feeling, and a grudge is the feeling with a name on it and a start date.

What is a grudge?

Wiktionary defines a grudge as "deep-seated and/or long-term animosity or ill will." Two things are built into that: it lasts, and it's aimed. You hold a grudge against someone, usually for something: "She still holds a grudge against him for the wedding speech."

A grudge is also countable. You can have one grudge, or a list of them, and you can say exactly when each one started. That's the whole premise of our app.

What is resentment?

Resentment is "anger or displeasure stemming from belief that one or one's group has been wronged or betrayed by others; indignation." It's a feeling, and it can be aimed at almost anything: a person, a decision, a rule, your pay, the way things turned out.

It's mostly used as an uncountable noun: you feel resentment, or a lot of it. The plural exists ("old resentments"), but it's less common than the singular.

Where the words come from

Grudge started out as a variant of the older English word grutch, from Middle English grucchen and Old French grouchier, meaning to murmur or grumble. It turns up in the mid-1400s. So the earliest grudge was a complaint muttered under the breath, which is still a fair description of many.

Resentment comes from resent, from French ressentir: re- plus sentir, "to feel." Early on, the English word could even mean gratitude or simply a strong impression. The narrow, bitter meaning won.

"Holding a grudge": what it means

To hold a grudge is to keep feeling ill will towards someone for something they did, long after it happened, and usually without letting it go when they would like you to. "Bear a grudge" means the same thing. "Nurse a grudge" adds that you're actively keeping it going.

A grudge holder is someone known for doing this. Whether that's an insult depends on who's saying it, and about what.

Grudge vs. begrudge

Despite the shared root, begrudge means something different: to envy someone something, or to give or do something reluctantly. "I don't begrudge her the promotion" means you aren't jealous. It doesn't say anything about a grudge, although in fairness it's often said by people who are.

Related words

  • Grievance: the wrong itself, or the complaint about it. A grudge is often built on a grievance.
  • Spite: wanting to hurt or annoy someone, often in a petty way. A grudge can lead to spite, but you can hold a grudge and do nothing at all.
  • Rancour (US: rancor): deep, long-lasting bitterness. Closer to resentment, at full strength.
  • Feud: a long-running, two-sided conflict, usually between families or groups. A grudge only needs one side. See the longest grudges in history for both kinds.

Examples

  • "He's held a grudge against the neighbours since they cut down the hedge." (specific, aimed, lasting: a grudge)
  • "There's a lot of resentment about the new rota." (a shared feeling about a situation: resentment)
  • "Her resentment hardened into a grudge." (the feeling, settling on a target)
  • "I don't begrudge him the money." (not envious, and not a grudge)
